\chapter{Results}
\label{cha:results}

This chapter presents the results of the performance evaluation of the 4G and 5G networks implemented in the testbed described in Chapter~\ref{cha:proposed-architecture}.

\section{General comparison between 4G and 5G performance}
\label{sec:4g-vs-5g}

This section presents a comparison of the 4G baseline against the 5G network across the three test rounds described in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, focusing on the evolution of performance metrics. Because the 5G configuration changed between rounds, namely the \gls{TDD} structure and the \texttt{rx\_gain} setting, these results are presented as a progression rather than a single snapshot, tracking how each change affected throughput at each location and in each direction. As will become clear over the following sections, no single round can be presented as the definitive 5G result: the configuration that performs best at short range is not the same as the one that performs best at long range, a trade-off examined in more detail later in this chapter.

In Figures \ref{fig:tp-tcp-dl} through \ref{fig:tp-udp-ul}, the mean throughput results for TCP and UDP in both downlink and uplink directions are presented for each test location, with black lines representing the range between Min/Max throughput values between each repetition for that combination. In Round~1 the comparison divides cleanly by direction. Over UDP the 5G downlink was ahead of the 4G baseline at every location, by roughly a factor of two, close to what the twofold difference in channel bandwidth alone would predict; over TCP the two generations were nearly level. The uplink went the other way, and by a far wider margin, falling to 0.6\,Mbps over TCP and 0.5\,Mbps over UDP at Location~C, 21 and 26 times below the 4G baseline. Part of that deficit was structural, as Section~\ref{sec:tdd-rebalance} shows.

Round~2 reversed the uplink result. With the rebalanced 2:2 \gls{TDD} pattern, the 5G uplink matched the 4G baseline at both of the locations measured, and the downlink lost between 22 and 56\% of its Round~1 throughput in exchange.

Round~3 separated the two ends of the room. Lowering \texttt{rx\_gain} to 60 roughly doubled the Location~A uplink again, to about twice the 4G baseline at that location. At Location~C the same change worked in reverse, leaving every measurement there, in both directions and over both protocols, below the 4G baseline. This is the trade-off referred to above, and Sections~\ref{sec:tdd-rebalance} and~\ref{sec:radio-link-quality} examine its two causes in turn.

\section{Unloaded latency}
\label{sec:unloaded-latency}

The throughput results describe how much data the network can carry, but not how quickly it responds when it is idle. To measure this, a \texttt{ping} command was used to send 30 ICMP echo requests at one-second intervals from the core machine to the \gls{UE}, at each of the three test locations, with no other traffic on the network. These measurements were taken during Round 3 only, with the 2:2 \gls{TDD} pattern and \texttt{rx\_gain} 60, and were not repeated for the 4G network, so the values in this section describe the 5G testbed latency only. Table~\ref{tab:unloaded-latency} presents the results.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{Loss} & \textbf{Min} & \textbf{Mean} & \textbf{Median} & \textbf{p95} & \textbf{Max} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m, line of sight) & 0\% & 16.0\,ms & 25.0\,ms & 24.6\,ms & 33.7\,ms & 34.9\,ms \\
        \hline
        B (6\,m, partition) & 0\% & 15.9\,ms & 28.1\,ms & 26.7\,ms & 41.6\,ms & 48.5\,ms \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m, concrete wall) & 0\% & 15.7\,ms & 26.2\,ms & 25.1\,ms & 35.3\,ms & 48.4\,ms \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Round 3 round-trip time from the core machine to the \gls{UE}, 30 ICMP echoes per location, with no other traffic on the network.}
    \label{tab:unloaded-latency}
\end{table}

Two results stand out. The first is that no packets were lost at any location, including through the concrete wall at Location C. The second is that the minimum round-trip time is around 15.7\,ms at all three locations. This is expected, as ten metres of air adds only 33\,ns in each direction, which is far below the measured floor, so propagation delay is irrelevant at these distances. The floor instead measures the fixed processing and scheduling overhead the packet passes through: the \gls{UE} modem, the \gls{gNB} \gls{PHY} and \gls{MAC} layers, the \gls{GTP-U} tunnel through the \gls{UPF}, and the same path back. This is the figure that best characterizes the latency of the testbed as deployed.

The means, at 25 to 28\,ms, sit well above that floor, and the spread between the minimum and the maximum across the three locations is wide. Plotting the round-trip time of each echo in sequence, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ping-sawtooth}, reveals a regular sawtooth in all locations, with the round-trip time falling steadily by 1 to 2\,ms from one echo to the next, then jumping back up, repeatedly throughout the ping test.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Ping_Sawtooth.pdf}
    \caption{Round-trip time of each of the 30 ICMP echoes at the three test locations, with the 15.7\,ms floor marked.}
    \label{fig:ping-sawtooth}
\end{figure}

The rate of descent is similar at Locations A and B, around 1.2\,ms per echo, and steeper at Location C, around 2.0\,ms per echo, a difference that lies in the ping tool rather than in the network. The locations also differ in how often the ramp restarts: Location A completes two long ramps over the 30 echoes, while Locations B and C restart more frequently and peak higher, reaching 48\,ms against Location A's 35\,ms. Those higher peaks exceed the 19\,ms jump measured at Location A, so at the weaker locations an echo occasionally waits more than one cycle, which is consistent with the weaker uplink signal at those locations under Round~3's \texttt{rx\_gain} of 60, examined later in this chapter.

The sawtooth is caused by a drift between the ping interval and the uplink scheduling cycle. Although \texttt{ping} was set to send one echo every 1000\,ms, the total interval duration reported in \texttt{ping}'s own summary, divided by the 29 intervals between the 30 echoes, gives 1001.24\,ms at Location A, 1001.28\,ms at Location B and 1001.97\,ms at Location C, most likely because the software timer on the core machine overshoots each interval by a small amount.

The uplink scheduling cycle comes from the way a \gls{UE} gains access to the uplink. In the downlink, the \gls{gNB} decides when to transmit, but a \gls{UE} with data to send and no uplink grant must first ask for one by sending a \gls{SR}, and it can only do so at periodic opportunities configured by the \gls{gNB}. Only once the request is received does the \gls{gNB} schedule the uplink transmission itself, on the \gls{PUSCH}. In srsRAN, the period of these opportunities is set by the \texttt{sr\_period\_ms} parameter of the \texttt{pucch} block, which defaults to 20\,ms and was not changed in this testbed's \texttt{gnb.yaml}, so the cell offered a scheduling request opportunity every 20\,ms throughout the campaign. As this cycle is driven by the \gls{gNB}'s radio, whose clock is locked to the \gls{GPSDO}, it repeats exactly every 20\,ms, and each echo reply generated by the \gls{UE} has to wait for the next opportunity before it can be sent. Because the ping interval is not an exact multiple of 20\,ms, each echo arrives later in the cycle than the one before it, by the amount the interval exceeds the one second mark. The time each reply waits for the next uplink opportunity therefore shrinks by that amount from one echo to the next, until an echo arrives just after an opportunity has passed and has to wait almost a full cycle, producing the jump. Table~\ref{tab:ping-drift} shows that the slope of the ramps in Figure~\ref{fig:ping-sawtooth} matches the excess of the ping interval over one second at every location, including the steeper ramp at Location C.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{Ping interval} & \textbf{Excess over 1000\,ms} & \textbf{Fitted ramp} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m) & 1001.24\,ms & 1.24\,ms & 1.23\,ms/echo \\
        \hline
        B (6\,m) & 1001.28\,ms & 1.28\,ms & 1.17\,ms/echo \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m) & 1001.97\,ms & 1.97\,ms & 2.03\,ms/echo \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Actual ping interval at each location, from the total duration in \texttt{ping}'s summary divided by 29 intervals, against the slope of the round-trip time ramp fitted from the 30 echoes.}
    \label{tab:ping-drift}
\end{table}

This proves that, despite \texttt{ping}'s time drift, the network maintains the 20\,ms scheduling cycle with precision. With a perfectly timed one-second interval, every echo would have arrived at the same point of the cycle, and the round-trip time would have been constant. By sweeping the echoes across the whole cycle, the drift shows that a packet the \gls{UE} has to send at an arbitrary moment waits anywhere between 0 and 20\,ms, about 10\,ms on average, for its next scheduling request opportunity. This is why the mean round-trip times, at 25 to 28\,ms, sit around 10\,ms above the 15.7\,ms floor.

Figure~\ref{fig:phase-shift} illustrates this mechanism with four consecutive echoes from the Location A \texttt{ping} series, echoes 23 to 26. Each bar is the time the echo's reply waits for the next scheduling request opportunity, taken as its measured round-trip time minus the 15.7\,ms floor. From one echo to the next, the reply becomes ready roughly 1.2\,ms later in the 20\,ms cycle, the amount by which Location A's ping interval exceeds one second, so the wait shrinks from 3.2\,ms to 2.0\,ms and then to 0.3\,ms. Echo 26 becomes ready just after the opportunity at 20\,ms has passed, and has to wait 19.2\,ms for the following one, which produces the jump from 16.0\,ms to 34.9\,ms seen in Figure~\ref{fig:ping-sawtooth}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{phase shift.pdf}
    \caption{Wait for the next scheduling request opportunity of four consecutive echo replies (echoes 23 to 26 of the Location A series), relative to the 20\,ms scheduling request cycle. Each wait is the echo's measured round-trip time minus the 15.7\,ms floor.}
    \label{fig:phase-shift}
\end{figure}

The waiting time explains the spread above the 15.7\,ms floor, but the scheduler's metrics also show where part of the floor itself goes. The \gls{gNB} reports the delay between receiving a scheduling request and the \gls{PUSCH} transmission it grants in its per-second scheduler metrics, an excerpt of which is shown in Listing~\ref{lst:sr-to-pusch}. Across all 630 one-second reports containing at least one scheduling request in the three Round~3 logs, this delay has a median of 7.5\,ms at every location and never exceeds 8\,ms.

\begin{lstlisting}[backgroundcolor=\color{gray!20},frame=single,framerule=0.8pt,rulecolor=\color{black},basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,columns=fixed,keepspaces=true,caption={Scheduling request fields from three consecutive \texttt{gnb.log} scheduler-metrics entries at Location A in Round~3.},label={lst:sr-to-pusch}]
17:39:53  sr_count=12  avg_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.79ms  max_sr_to_pusch_delay=8ms
17:39:54  sr_count=3   avg_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.5ms   max_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.5ms
17:39:55  sr_count=13  avg_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.5ms   max_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.5ms
\end{lstlisting}

Roughly half of the floor is therefore dedicated to the uplink access procedure itself, with 7.5\,ms between the scheduling request and the uplink transmission, and the remaining 8.2\,ms covering the downlink leg, the \gls{UE}'s processing of the echo request, and the return path through the \gls{gNB} and the \gls{UPF} to the core machine. Putting the section together, the idle round-trip time of the testbed is made up of this fixed 15.7\,ms, plus between 0 and 20\,ms spent waiting for the next scheduling request opportunity, determined by when the packet happens to arrive in the cycle rather than by how far it travels.

One qualification applies to all of the figures in this section: they describe an idle network.
Once a transfer is running, the round-trip time rises by more than an order of magnitude, as the next section shows.

\section{Latency under load}
\label{sec:bufferbloat}

The round-trip times in Section~\ref{sec:unloaded-latency} describe the network with no other traffic on it. The throughput tests, however, give a different picture. In a downlink TCP test, the core machine is the sender, so the \texttt{iperf3} server records the round-trip time that the TCP stack measures for each stream, along with the number of retransmissions. Table~\ref{tab:loaded-latency} summarizes these statistics for the Round~3 TCP downlink tests, which were run in the same session as the idle measurements.

As soon as a download starts, the mean round-trip time rises from 25--28\,ms to between 0.2 and 2.3\,s, with individual samples exceeding 5\,s at Location C. The radio link is the same one measured in the previous section, so this added delay is not caused by propagation or processing, but by the time the packets spend waiting in a queue.

That queue is inside the \gls{gNB}. Each data radio bearer has a downlink \gls{RLC} transmit queue, sized when the bearer is set up. Listing~\ref{lst:rlc-queue} shows the configuration the \gls{gNB} printed when the \gls{UE} attached.

\begin{lstlisting}[backgroundcolor=\color{gray!20},frame=single,framerule=0.8pt,rulecolor=\color{black},basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,columns=fixed,keepspaces=true,caption={Downlink data radio bearer configuration from \texttt{gnb.log} at Location A in Round~3.},label={lst:rlc-queue}]
[PDCP] DRB1 DL: PDCP configured. rlc_mode=AM discard_timer=infinity
[RLC ] DRB1 DL: RLC AM configured. queue_size=16384 queue_size_bytes=6172672
\end{lstlisting}

In the radio configuration, the queue holds up to 6.17\,MB of downlink data, and with the \gls{PDCP} discard timer set to infinity, nothing is removed from it for being too old. At the 22\,Mbps reached at Location A, 6.17\,MB takes about 2.2\,s to transmit, and at Location C's 7\,Mbps, about 7\,s. TCP increases its sending rate until it detects packet loss, and as long as the queue has room, packets keep accumulating and none is lost, so TCP keeps sending more data into it. The \gls{gNB}'s own metrics confirm this: the downlink buffer it reports during these tests averages between 1.7 and 4.5\,MB at Location A and between 2.3 and 3.3\,MB at Location C.

At Location A, in two of the three repetitions, the round-trip time climbs steadily for around 14 seconds, then levels off at approximately 2\,s, the time needed to empty a full queue at that throughput. At the same moment, retransmissions appear for the first time, in bursts of several hundred per second as the queue gets full, new packets are dropped and only then does TCP slow down. The \gls{gNB}'s log confirms this directly: it records packets being dropped with the cause ``\gls{RLC} SDU queue is full'' starting 12 to 13 seconds into both of these tests, within a second of the retransmissions beginning. Listing~\ref{lst:rlc-drop} shows the moment the first drop occurs in the second repetition: the last packet accepted brings the queue to 6.08\,MB, 98.6\% of its limit, and the next packets are discarded.

\begin{lstlisting}[backgroundcolor=\color{gray!20},frame=single,framerule=0.8pt,rulecolor=\color{black},basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,columns=fixed,keepspaces=true,caption={First queue-full drop in \texttt{gnb.log} during the second Location A TCP downlink test in Round~3, 13.6 seconds into the test, shortened to the relevant fields.},label={lst:rlc-drop}]
17:40:35.600971 [RLC ] DRB1 DL: TX SDU. queued_sdus=4337 queued_bytes=6084811
17:40:35.601196 [PDCP] DRB1 DL: Dropping SDU. Cause: RLC SDU queue is full
17:40:35.603092 [PDCP] DRB1 DL: Drop burst finished. nof_sdus=15
\end{lstlisting}

At Location C, the link is slow enough that the queue only fills in the last seconds of the test, with the first drops logged 18 to 21 seconds in, too late for TCP to detect the losses before the test ends. The round-trip time climbs for the whole test, from 391\,ms to 4.9\,s in the second repetition, without a single retransmission, so TCP never receives the signal that would make it slow down.

This result affects how the downlink throughput figures in Section~\ref{sec:4g-vs-5g} should be read. The \gls{gNB} used the same 6.17\,MB queue in Round~2, so the 5G TCP downlink figures from both Rounds~2 and~3 were obtained with seconds of queueing delay. The throughput is real, but any interactive application, such as a video call or streaming, sharing the radio link with a download would experience such delay, in the form of buffering or stuttering.

This is not a limitation of the 5G radio, but of the \gls{gNB}'s queue size, which, when not set in its configuration file, defaults to the value reported above. A smaller queue could keep latency low under load at little cost to throughput. Admittedly, this change was identified after the final test round and was not tested, so it is left as future work.

\section{TDD rebalance effects on uplink airtime and throughput}
\label{sec:tdd-rebalance}

Returning to the throughput results of Section~\ref{sec:4g-vs-5g}, the remaining sections examine the configuration factors behind them. One of the problems identified in the first round of testing was that the 5G uplink throughput was capped by the \gls{TDD} structure configuration implemented in the testbed, which, as mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:experimental-methodology}, allocated \gls{TDD} timeslots disproportionately in favor of the downlink. The figures in the section show that with this configuration, the 5G downlink received 68.6\% of the total airtime, compared with 22.9\% for the uplink.

We can verify this asymmetry by examining the raw metrics from the \gls{gNB} trace files, checking the scheduler's actual uplink grant rate against the theoretical maximum allowed by the \gls{TDD} configuration.

In the radio configuration file, shown in Listing~\ref{lst:cell_cfg}, the \texttt{cell\_cfg} block sets the \gls{SCS} to 30\,kHz. As explained in Chapter~\ref{cha:5g-fundamentals}, at this \gls{SCS}, a slot is 0.5\,ms long, and the 3:1 \gls{TDD} pattern sets a 5-slot transmission period with one uplink slot, giving a total period of 2.5\,ms. This means that the maximum number of uplink slots that can be scheduled per second is $1000\,\text{ms} / 2.5\,\text{ms} = 400$ uplink slots, regardless of the radio link quality.

cell_cfg:
  dl_arfcn: 627340
  band: 78
  channel_bandwidth_MHz: 20
  common_scs: 30
  plmn: "00101"
  tac: 7
  pci: 1
\end{lstlisting}

The columns of interest here are \texttt{ok} and \texttt{nok}: the number of successful and failed uplink transmissions the scheduler reports for that one-second period. The remaining columns (\gls{SINR}, \gls{RSRP}, and \gls{MCS}) speak to link quality rather than to the \gls{TDD} structure itself.

The \texttt{ok} and \texttt{nok} columns sum to $382+18=400$ transmissions for the first second, as do the following seconds. Iterating over the full Round 1 run and discarding the initial TCP slow-start period (considering only seconds where the grant count reaches at least 90\% of the structural ceiling), this averages to 400.0 grants/s at Location A and 399.7 grants/s at Location C, matching the same maximum established before. The 4G baseline, for comparison, measured the same way from the \gls{eNB} traces, grants 1000.0 and 992.5 subframes/s at the same two locations, respectively. This equates to a 2.5$\times$ reduction in uplink transmission opportunities ($1000.0/400.0 = 2.5$ at Location A, $992.5/399.7 = 2.48$ at Location C) between 4G and 5G performance, which no amount of signal quality could recover, since it is imposed before a single symbol is transmitted.

In the subsequent rounds of testing, the \gls{TDD} pattern was rebalanced to a symmetrical 2:2 pattern, expected to improve uplink throughput at the cost of downlink throughput, making their performance almost equal, which Figure~\ref{fig:tdd-structure} presents. Only one configuration variable changes between each pair of rounds in the figure (the \gls{TDD} pattern between rounds 1 and 2, and \texttt{rx\_gain} between rounds 2 and 3) so the two steps should be read as evidence for two separate effects rather than a single continuous trend.

The pattern used from Round~2 onward, shown in Listing~\ref{lst:tdd-22-cfg} of Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology}, doubles \texttt{nof\_ul\_slots} to 2 within the same 5-slot, 2.5\,ms period, raising the structural ceiling to $2\times400=800$ uplink transmissions per second. Round~2 metrics data was extracted directly from srsRAN's native timestamped scheduler log rather than the periodic console dump used in Round~1, so the raw evidence takes a different shape: Listing~\ref{lst:ul-800-grant-ceiling} shows an excerpt of the relevant uplink fields from three consecutive one-second \texttt{gnb.log} entries for the Location A TCP uplink test (the full lines also report downlink and per-HARQ statistics, omitted here for space).

The \texttt{ul\_nof\_ok} and \texttt{ul\_nof\_nok} fields sum to exactly 800 in every row shown, and the same holds throughout the saturated portion of the run at both Location A and Location C, confirming the scheduler reaches the full doubled ceiling regardless of distance. This is a direct consequence of the adjustment in the \gls{TDD} pattern, which is what the grant rate alone predicts.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TDD_structure_comparison.pdf}
    \caption{TCP throughput at Locations A (2\,m) and C (10\,m) across the three 5G
    rounds. The x-axis shows the specific configuration used in each round.}
    \label{fig:tdd-structure}
\end{figure}

We see on the figure that initially, as expected, when using the 3:1 \gls{TDD} pattern, the uplink throughput on both Location A and C was significantly lower than the downlink throughput. On Round 2, after rebalancing the \gls{TDD} pattern to 2:2, the uplink performance rose to nearly match the downlink in Location A, surpassing the downlink throughput at Location C. We also note that the downlink throughput decreased in both locations, as expected.

The same airtime accounting also predicts the downlink share should fall $\times0.69$ of its Round 1 value; the observed downlink throughput fell further still, to $\times0.56$ at Location A and $\times0.44$ at Location C, so some factor beyond airtime alone also affected the downlink.

Finally, on Round 3, after adjusting the \texttt{rx\_gain} to 60: at Location A (2\,m), the overall network throughput increased significantly, as this change removed the receiver overload reported at that location, thus stabilizing the network's performance. However, at Location C, further away from the radio, the uplink throughput decreased significantly as well, due to the lower \texttt{rx\_gain} desensitizing the receiver and reducing the link budget. This change was expected as a trade-off between the two locations, and it highlights the challenge of finding a single configuration that optimizes performance across all distances.

We also see that in this final configuration, the throughput between both directions is closest to being equal at short range: at Location A the two directions converge to within a narrow margin (22.4 vs.\ 25.2\,Mbps), the best balance observed anywhere in the dataset, while at Location C the \texttt{rx\_gain} reduction gives back most of the uplink gain Round 2 had recovered. This Location C behaviour is the trade-off associated with the \texttt{rx\_gain} change.


\section{Radio link quality}
\label{sec:radio-link-quality}

The previous section showed that the \gls{TDD} pattern limits how often the 5G uplink can transmit. How much data each transmission carries depends on the quality of the radio link, which this section examines through two metrics the \gls{RAN} reports for every uplink transmission: the \gls{SINR} measured by the base station, and the power headroom reported by the \gls{UE}. The power headroom is the difference between the \gls{UE}'s maximum transmit power and the power it is currently using, so a positive value means the \gls{UE} still has power in reserve, and a negative value means it is being asked for more power than it can deliver.

\subsection{Round 1: 4G and 5G}

Table~\ref{tab:r1-link-quality} compares both metrics for the two generations in Round~1, averaged over the uplink tests at each location.

At Location A, both networks see a similar uplink \gls{SINR}. With increasing distance, however, the two behave very differently: the 4G \gls{SINR} stays between 12 and 14.3\,dB at every location, while the 5G \gls{SINR} falls by more than 18\,dB between Locations A and C. The power headroom explains the difference. The 4G \gls{UE} keeps between 10 and 17\,dB of transmit power in reserve at every location, so as the path loss grows, the \gls{eNB}'s power control can ask it to transmit harder and hold the \gls{SINR} roughly constant. The 5G \gls{UE}, on the other hand, has no headroom left even at Location A, and is well beyond its maximum at Locations B and C, so every additional decibel of path loss is lost directly from the received \gls{SINR}.

Part of this difference is expected, as the 5G \gls{RAN} operates at a higher frequency, 3.4\,GHz against 2.6\,GHz, and without the low-noise amplifier used on the 4G receive chain. However, why the 5G \gls{UE} runs out of headroom this early was not established.
\subsection{Rounds 2 and 3: the receiver gain trade-off}

Between Round~2 and Round~3, the 5G uplink was also affected by the receive gain of the radio, set by the \texttt{rx\_gain} parameter. Table~\ref{tab:rxgain-link-quality} compares the uplink at each location with \texttt{rx\_gain} at 70, in Round~2, and at 60, in Round~3, with the same 2:2 \gls{TDD} pattern in both. Location B is only shown for Round~3, as its Round~2 data was excluded (Section~\ref{sec:test-round-2}).

At \texttt{rx\_gain} 70, the two ends of the room show an inconsistency. The \gls{gNB} reports a similar \gls{SINR} at Locations A and C, 20.5 and 18.0\,dB, yet at Location A, the closest to the radio, almost a quarter of the uplink transmissions fail, against 1.3\% at Location C. A \gls{SINR} reading that high, specifically paired with such high \gls{BLER}, is the signature of an overloaded receiver, as the distortion produced by the saturated front end is not recognized as noise, so the reported \gls{SINR} stays high, making the scheduler pick an \gls{MCS} that the distorted signal cannot support.

The radio's own \texttt{ovl} flag, which srsRAN reports whenever the receiver is saturated, supports this reading. The \gls{gNB} writes one metrics line per second for as long as the \gls{UE} is attached, so they cover the whole session at each location, the twelve tests and the intervals between them. At \texttt{rx\_gain} 70, the flag was raised 28 times at Location A and 12 times at Location B, and never at Location C. Of the 28 at Location A, six were raised while the uplink was carrying between 6 and 10\,Mbps and four during downlink transfers, several of them with uplink error rates between 25 and 100\%.

Lowering \texttt{rx\_gain} to 60 in Round~3 removed the overload at Location A, where the flag was raised only three times over a session of comparable length. The uplink \gls{BLER} fell from 22.4 to 6.6\%, and the throughput doubled to 28.5\,Mbps, the highest uplink throughput measured in the testbed. At Location C, the same change had the opposite effect: the \gls{SINR} fell by 17\,dB, to just above the noise floor, and the throughput fell to less than a fifth of its Round~2 value.

The power headroom shows why the same 10\,dB reduction in receive gain helped one location and harmed the other. With less gain in the receiver, the \gls{gNB}'s power control asks the \gls{UE} to transmit harder to compensate. At Location A, its headroom fell by 11\,dB, almost exactly the gain removed from the receiver, so the received signal recovered, now without overloading the receiver. At Location C, the \gls{UE} was already asked for more than its maximum power in Round~2, with a headroom of $-16.1$\,dB, and had no power left to give, so the gain reduction was lost directly from the received \gls{SINR}.

With a single, manually set receive gain and no automatic gain control, the \gls{gNB} cannot serve the whole room. A gain of 70 suits Location C and overloads the receiver at Location A, while a gain of 60 suits Location A and leaves Location C barely above the noise floor. This is a consequence of running a software \gls{RAN} on an \gls{SDR} whose receive gain is fixed at start-up.

\section{Core network performance}
\label{sec:core-performance}

The core network is involved in the data path only through the \gls{UPF}, whose contribution to the round-trip time is part of the 15.7\,ms floor measured in Section~\ref{sec:unloaded-latency}. Its main role is in the control plane: registering the \gls{UE}, authenticating it, and setting up the session through which it reaches the data network. The time this takes can be measured directly from the Open5GS logs, which record every registration request received by the \gls{AMF}, and every attach request received by the \gls{MME}, together with the moment the procedure completes. Table~\ref{tab:registration-time} summarizes these times over the three test sessions.

The same intervals can also be measured from the outside, in the \gls{NGAP} captures taken at the \gls{gNB}, which record the procedure from the radio side rather than from within the core. These files can be opened through Wireshark, and reveal that 23 registrations appear in these captures. For each one of them, the time from the \texttt{InitialUEMessage} carrying the registration request to the message with which the \gls{AMF} closes the procedure matches with the interval in the Core's \gls{AMF} log to within 2\,ms, on a different machine and a different clock.

A 5G registration took a median of 186\,ms. The six slowest share two properties, both visible in the \gls{AMF} log. The first is that the \gls{AMF} had to set up its connections to the \gls{AUSF}, \gls{UDM} and \gls{PCF} during the registration rather than reuse existing ones: in the \gls{SBA}, a network function does not know in advance where the others are, so it first asks the \gls{NRF} for them, and then connects to each one. Timing this directly, from the \gls{AMF}'s request for each network function to the endpoint being established, gives between 12 and 16\,ms for all three together.

The second property accounts for most of the difference: in these six registrations the \gls{AMF} held no security context for the \gls{UE}, so the procedure ran a full authentication, exchanging an identity request, an authentication request and a security mode command with the \gls{UE}, each one a round trip over the air. One of the six, the slowest registration of the campaign, was also captured on the \gls{NGAP} interface at the \gls{gNB}, and Figure~\ref{fig:ngap-registration} places it next to a registration that reused a context, so that the two can be followed frame by frame.

In the upper capture, the registration request arrives in frame 3 and the \gls{AMF} accepts it in frame 10, the \texttt{InitialContextSetupRequest} that carries the registration accept. The six frames in between are the authentication: an identity request and its response, an authentication request and its response, and a security mode command and its completion. Together they take 178 of the registration's 383\,ms, and each is a round trip to the \gls{UE} over the radio, which is why they dominate the total. In the lower capture those six frames are absent, and the accept follows the request as frame 4, a single millisecond later. The cost specific to the \gls{SBA} is therefore around 13\,ms, paid only on first use, while the rest of the spread in Table~\ref{tab:registration-time} is the ordinary cost of authenticating a \gls{UE}, which 4G pays as well.
