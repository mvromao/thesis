\section{General comparison between 4G and 5G performance}
\label{sec:4g-vs-5g}

This section presents a comparison of the 4G baseline against the 5G network across the three test rounds described in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, focusing on the evolution of performance metrics. As will become clear over the following sections, no single round can be presented as the definitive 5G result: the configuration that performs best at short range is not the same as the one that performs best at long range, a trade-off examined in more detail later in this chapter.

\section{Unloaded latency}
\label{sec:unloaded-latency}

The throughput results describe how much data the network can carry, but not how quickly it responds when it is idle. To measure this, a \texttt{ping} command was used to send 30 ICMP echo requests at one-second intervals from the core machine to the \gls{UE}, at each of the three test locations, with no other traffic on the network. These measurements were taken during Round 3 only, with the 2:2 TDD pattern and \texttt{rx\_gain} 60, and were not repeated for the 4G network, so the values in this section describe the 5G testbed latency only. Table~\ref{tab:unloaded-latency} presents the results.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{Loss} & \textbf{Min} & \textbf{Mean} & \textbf{Median} & \textbf{p95} & \textbf{Max} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m, line of sight) & 0\% & 15.7\,ms & 25.0\,ms & 24.6\,ms & 33.7\,ms & 34.9\,ms \\
        \hline
        B (6\,m, partition) & 0\% & 15.8\,ms & 28.1\,ms & 26.7\,ms & 41.6\,ms & 48.5\,ms \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m, concrete wall) & 0\% & 15.7\,ms & 26.2\,ms & 25.1\,ms & 35.3\,ms & 48.4\,ms \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Round 3 round-trip time from the core machine to the \gls{UE}, 30 ICMP echoes per location, with no other traffic on the network.}
    \label{tab:unloaded-latency}
\end{table}

Two results stand out. The first is that no packets were lost at any location, including through the concrete wall at Location C. The second is that the minimum round-trip time is around 15.7\,ms at all three locations. This is expected, as ten metres of air adds only 33\,ns in each direction, which is far below the measured floor, so propagation delay is irrelevant at these distances. The floor instead measures the fixed processing and scheduling overhead the packet passes through: the \gls{UE} modem, the \gls{gNB} PHY and MAC layers, the GTP-U tunnel through the \gls{UPF}, and the same path back. This is the figure that best characterizes the latency of the testbed as deployed.

The means, at 25 to 28\,ms, sit well above that floor, and the spread between the minimum and the maximum across the three locations is wide. Plotting the round-trip time of each echo in sequence, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ping-sawtooth}, reveals a regular sawtooth in all locations, with the round-trip time falling steadily by 1 to 2\,ms from one echo to the next, then jumping back up, repeatedly throughout the ping test. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Ping_Sawtooth.pdf}
    \caption{Round-trip time of each of the 30 ICMP echoes at the three test locations, with the 15.7\,ms floor marked.}
    \label{fig:ping-sawtooth}
\end{figure}

The rate of descent is similar at Locations A and B, around 1.2\,ms per echo, and steeper at Location C, around 2.0\,ms per echo, a difference that lies in the ping tool rather than in the network. The locations also differ in how often the ramp restarts: Location A completes two long ramps over the 30 echoes, while Locations B and C restart more frequently and peak higher, reaching 48\,ms against Location A's 35\,ms. Those higher peaks exceed the 19\,ms jump measured at Location A, so at the weaker locations an echo occasionally waits more than one cycle, which is consistent with the weaker uplink signal at those locations under Round~3's \texttt{rx\_gain} of 60, examined later in this chapter.

The sawtooth is caused by a drift between the ping interval and the uplink scheduling cycle. Although \texttt{ping} was set to send one echo every 1000\,ms, the total interval duration reported in \texttt{ping}'s own summary, divided by the 29 intervals between the 30 echoes, gives 1001.24\,ms at Location A, 1001.28\,ms at Location B and 1001.97\,ms at Location C, most likely because the software timer on the core machine overshoots each interval by a small amount.

The uplink scheduling cycle comes from the way a \gls{UE} gains access to the uplink. In the downlink, the \gls{gNB} decides when to transmit, but a \gls{UE} with data to send and no uplink grant must first ask for one by sending a \gls{SR}, and it can only do so at periodic opportunities configured by the \gls{gNB}. Only once the request is received does the \gls{gNB} schedule the uplink transmission itself, on the \gls{PUSCH}. In srsRAN, the period of these opportunities is set by the \texttt{sr\_period\_ms} parameter of the \texttt{pucch} block, which defaults to 20\,ms and was not changed in this testbed's \texttt{gnb.yaml}, so the cell offered a scheduling request opportunity every 20\,ms throughout the campaign. As this cycle is driven by the \gls{gNB}'s radio, whose clock is locked to the \gls{GPSDO}, it repeats exactly every 20\,ms, and each echo reply generated by the \gls{UE} has to wait for the next opportunity before it can be sent. Because the ping interval is not an exact multiple of 20\,ms, each echo arrives later in the cycle than the one before it, by the amount the interval exceeds the one second mark. The time each reply waits for the next uplink opportunity therefore shrinks by that amount from one echo to the next, until an echo arrives just after an opportunity has passed and has to wait almost a full cycle, producing the jump. Table~\ref{tab:ping-drift} shows that the slope of the ramps in Figure~\ref{fig:ping-sawtooth} matches the excess of the ping interval over one second at every location, including the steeper ramp at Location C.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{Ping interval} & \textbf{Excess over 1000\,ms} & \textbf{Fitted ramp} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m) & 1001.24\,ms & 1.24\,ms & 1.23\,ms/echo \\
        \hline
        B (6\,m) & 1001.28\,ms & 1.28\,ms & 1.17\,ms/echo \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m) & 1001.97\,ms & 1.97\,ms & 2.03\,ms/echo \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Actual ping interval at each location, from the total duration in \texttt{ping}'s summary divided by 29 intervals, against the slope of the round-trip time ramp fitted from the 30 echoes.}
    \label{tab:ping-drift}
\end{table}

This proves that, despite \texttt{ping}'s time drift, the network maintains the 20\,ms scheduling cycle with precision. With a perfectly timed one-second interval, every echo would have arrived at the same point of the cycle, and the round-trip time would have been constant. By sweeping the echoes across the whole cycle, the drift shows that a packet the \gls{UE} has to send at an arbitrary moment waits anywhere between 0 and 20\,ms, about 10\,ms on average, for its next scheduling request opportunity. This is why the mean round-trip times, at 25 to 28\,ms, sit around 10\,ms above the 15.7\,ms floor.

Figure~\ref{fig:phase-shift} illustrates this mechanism with four consecutive echoes from the Location A \texttt{ping} series, echoes 23 to 26. Each bar is the time the echo's reply waits for the next scheduling request opportunity, taken as its measured round-trip time minus the 15.7\,ms floor. From one echo to the next, the reply becomes ready roughly 1.2\,ms later in the 20\,ms cycle, the amount by which Location A's ping interval exceeds one second, so the wait shrinks from 3.2\,ms to 2.0\,ms and then to 0.3\,ms. Echo 26 becomes ready just after the opportunity at 20\,ms has passed, and has to wait 19.2\,ms for the following one, which produces the jump from 16.0\,ms to 34.9\,ms seen in Figure~\ref{fig:ping-sawtooth}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{phase shift.pdf}
    \caption{Wait for the next scheduling request opportunity of four consecutive echo replies (echoes 23 to 26 of the Location A series), relative to the 20\,ms scheduling request cycle. Each wait is the echo's measured round-trip time minus the 15.7\,ms floor.}
    \label{fig:phase-shift}
\end{figure}

The waiting time explains the spread above the 15.7\,ms floor, but the scheduler's metrics also show where part of the floor itself goes. The \gls{gNB} reports the delay between receiving a scheduling request and the \gls{PUSCH} transmission it grants in its per-second scheduler metrics, an excerpt of which is shown in Listing~\ref{lst:sr-to-pusch}. Across all 630 one-second reports containing at least one scheduling request in the three Round~3 logs, this delay has a median of 7.5\,ms at every location and never exceeds 8\,ms.

\begin{lstlisting}[backgroundcolor=\color{gray!20},frame=single,framerule=0.8pt,rulecolor=\color{black},basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,columns=fixed,keepspaces=true,caption={Scheduling request fields from three consecutive \texttt{gnb.log} scheduler-metrics entries at Location A in Round~3.},label={lst:sr-to-pusch}]
17:39:53  sr_count=12  avg_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.79ms  max_sr_to_pusch_delay=8ms
17:39:54  sr_count=3   avg_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.5ms   max_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.5ms
17:39:55  sr_count=13  avg_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.5ms   max_sr_to_pusch_delay=7.5ms
\end{lstlisting}

Roughly half of the floor is therefore dedicated to the uplink access procedure itself, with 7.5\,ms between the scheduling request and the uplink transmission, and the remaining 8.2\,ms covering the downlink leg, the \gls{UE}'s processing of the echo request, and the return path through the \gls{gNB} and the \gls{UPF} to the core machine. Putting the section together, the idle round-trip time of the testbed is made up of this fixed 15.7\,ms, plus between 0 and 20\,ms spent waiting for the next scheduling request opportunity, determined by when the packet happens to arrive in the cycle rather than by how far it travels.

One qualification applies to all of the figures in this section: they describe an idle network. 
Once a transfer is running, the round-trip time rises by more than an order of magnitude, as the next section shows. 

\section{Latency under load}
\label{sec:bufferbloat}

The round-trip times in Section~\ref{sec:unloaded-latency} describe the network with no other traffic on it. The throughput tests, however, give a different picture. In a downlink TCP test, the core machine is the sender, so the \texttt{iperf3} server records the round-trip time that the TCP stack measures for each stream, along with the number of retransmissions. Table~\ref{tab:loaded-latency} summarises these statistics for the Round~3 TCP downlink tests, which were run in the same session as the idle measurements.


\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{Throughput} & \textbf{\makecell{Mean RTT\\(range)}} & \textbf{Max RTT} & \textbf{\makecell{Retrans-\\missions}} & \textbf{\makecell{Idle mean\\RTT}} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m) & 21.1--24.0\,Mb/s & \makecell{981\,ms\\(529--1213)} & 1.3--2.2\,s & 242--2240 & 25.0\,ms \\
        \hline
        B (6\,m) & 9.2--17.0\,Mb/s & \makecell{1272\,ms\\(230--2011)} & 0.7--4.2\,s & 108--767 & 28.1\,ms \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m) & 6.1--7.3\,Mb/s & \makecell{2133\,ms\\(1995--2255)} & 3.5--5.3\,s & 0--54 & 26.2\,ms \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Round~3 TCP downlink tests at each location: the mean round-trip time reported by the \texttt{iperf3} server, averaged over the three repetitions, with the lowest and highest repetition in brackets; and the range across the three repetitions of the throughput, the maximum round-trip time, and the number of retransmissions, with the idle mean round-trip time from Table~\ref{tab:unloaded-latency} for comparison.}
    \label{tab:loaded-latency}
\end{table}

As soon as a download starts, the mean round-trip time rises from 25--28\,ms to between 0.2 and 2.3\,s, with individual samples exceeding 5\,s at Location C. The radio link is the same one measured in the previous section, so this added delay is not caused by propagation or processing, it is time the packets spend waiting in a queue.

That queue is inside the \gls{gNB}. Each data radio bearer has a downlink \gls{RLC} transmit queue, sized when the bearer is set up. Listing~\ref{lst:rlc-queue} shows the configuration the \gls{gNB} printed when the \gls{UE} attached.

\begin{lstlisting}[backgroundcolor=\color{gray!20},frame=single,framerule=0.8pt,rulecolor=\color{black},basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,columns=fixed,keepspaces=true,caption={Downlink data radio bearer configuration from \texttt{gnb.log} at Location A in Round~3.},label={lst:rlc-queue}]
[PDCP] DRB1 DL: PDCP configured. rlc_mode=AM discard_timer=infinity
[RLC ] DRB1 DL: RLC AM configured. queue_size=16384 queue_size_bytes=6172672
\end{lstlisting}

In the radio configuration, the queue holds up to 6.17\,MB of downlink data, and with the \gls{PDCP} discard timer set to infinity, nothing is removed from it for being too old. At the 22\,Mb/s reached at Location A, 6.17\,MB takes about 2.2\,s to transmit, and at Location C's 7\,Mb/s, about 7\,s. TCP increases its sending rate until it detects packet loss, and as long as the queue has room, packets keep accumulating and none is lost, so TCP keeps sending more data into it. The \gls{gNB}'s own metrics confirm this: the downlink buffer it reports during these tests averages between 1.7 and 4.5\,MB at Location A and between 2.3 and 3.3\,MB at Location C.

At Location A, in two of the three repetitions, the round-trip time climbs steadily for around 14 seconds, then levels off at approximately 2\,s, the time needed to empty a full queue at that throughput. At the same moment, retransmissions appear for the first time, in bursts of several hundred per second as the queue gets full, new packets are dropped and only then TCP does slow down. The \gls{gNB}'s log confirms this directly: it records packets being dropped with the cause ``RLC SDU queue is full'' starting 12 to 13 seconds into both of these tests, within a second of the retransmissions beginning. Listing~\ref{lst:rlc-drop} shows the moment the first drop occurs in the second repetition: the last packet accepted brings the queue to 6.08\,MB, 98.6\% of its limit, and the next packets are discarded. 

\begin{lstlisting}[backgroundcolor=\color{gray!20},frame=single,framerule=0.8pt,rulecolor=\color{black},basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,columns=fixed,keepspaces=true,caption={First queue-full drop in \texttt{gnb.log} during the second Location A TCP downlink test in Round~3, 13.6 seconds into the test, shortened to the relevant fields.},label={lst:rlc-drop}]
17:40:35.600971 [RLC ] DRB1 DL: TX SDU. queued_sdus=4337 queued_bytes=6084811
17:40:35.601196 [PDCP] DRB1 DL: Dropping SDU. Cause: RLC SDU queue is full
17:40:35.603092 [PDCP] DRB1 DL: Drop burst finished. nof_sdus=15
\end{lstlisting}

At Location C, the link is slow enough that the queue only fills in the last seconds of the test, with the first drops logged 18 to 21 seconds in, too late for TCP to detect the losses before the test ends. The round-trip time climbs for the whole test, from 391\,ms to 4.9\,s in the second repetition, without a single retransmission, so TCP never receives the signal that would make it slow down.

This result affects how the downlink throughput figures in Section~\ref{sec:4g-vs-5g} should be read. The \gls{gNB} used the same 6.17\,MB queue in Round~2, so the 5G TCP downlink figures from both Rounds~2 and~3 were obtained with seconds of queueing delay. The throughput is real, but any interactive application, such as a video call or streaming, sharing the radio link with a download would experience such delay, in the form of buffering or stuttering. 

This is not a limitation of the 5G radio, but of the \gls{gNB}'s queue size, which when not set in its configuration file, defaults to the value reported above. A smaller queue could keep latency low under load at little cost to throughput. Admittedly, this change was identified after the final test round and was not tested, so it is left as future work.

\section{TDD rebalance effects on uplink airtime and throughput}
\label{sec:tdd-rebalance}

Returning to the throughput results of Section~\ref{sec:4g-vs-5g}, the remaining sections examine the configuration factors behind them. One of the troubles identified in the first round of testing was that the 5G uplink throughput was capped by the TDD structure configuration implemented in the testbed, which, as mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:experimental-methodology}, allocated TDD timeslots disproportionately in favor of the downlink. The figures in the section show that with this configuration, the 5G downlink received 68.8\% of the total airtime, compared with 22.9\% for the uplink.

We can verify this asymmetry by examining the raw metrics from the gNB trace files, checking the scheduler's actual uplink grant rate against the theoretical maximum allowed by the TDD configuration.

In the radio configuration file, shown in Listing~\ref{lst:cell_cfg}, the \texttt{cell\_cfg} block sets the \gls{SCS} to 30\,kHz. As explained in Chapter~\ref{cha:5g-fundamentals}, at this \gls{SCS}, a slot is 0.5\,ms long, and the 3:1 TDD pattern sets a 5-slot transmission period with one uplink slot, giving a total period of 2.5\,ms. This means that the maximum number of uplink slots that can be scheduled per second is $1000\,\text{ms} / 2.5\,\text{ms} = 400$ uplink slots, regardless of the radio link quality.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=yaml,backgroundcolor=\color{gray!20},frame=single,framerule=0.8pt,rulecolor=\color{black},basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,caption={\texttt{gnb.yaml} \texttt{cell\_cfg} block used in Round 1.},label={lst:cell_cfg}]
cell_cfg:
  dl_arfcn: 627340
  band: 78
  channel_bandwidth_MHz: 20
  common_scs: 30
  plmn: "00101"
  tac: 7
  pci: 1
\end{lstlisting}

Listing~\ref{lst:ul-grant-ceiling} shows an excerpt of the raw uplink metrics from one of the Round~1 gNB trace files at Location A, taken while a TCP uplink transfer was saturating the link. The columns of interest here are \texttt{ok} and \texttt{nok}: the number of successful and failed uplink transmissions the scheduler reports for that one-second period. The remaining columns (SINR, RSRP, and MCS) speak to link quality rather than to the TDD structure itself.

\begin{lstlisting}[backgroundcolor=\color{gray!20},frame=single,framerule=0.8pt,rulecolor=\color{black},basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,columns=fixed,keepspaces=true,caption={Console trace from the Location A (2\,m) TCP uplink test in Round~1.},label={lst:ul-grant-ceiling}]
|-------------------------------UL-----------------------------
| pusch  rsrp  ri  mcs  brate   ok  nok  (%)    bsr     ta  phr
|  18.3 -17.0   1   14  7.77M  382   18   4%   700k   240n    3
|  19.1 -16.2   1   15  7.68M  376   24   6%   700k   227n    0
|  19.7 -15.6   1   16  6.25M  308   92  23%   700k   234n   -3
\end{lstlisting}

The \texttt{ok} and \texttt{nok} columns sum to $382+18=400$ transmissions for the first second, as do the following seconds. Iterating over the full Round 1 run and discarding the initial TCP slow-start period (considering only seconds where the grant count reaches at least 90\% of the structural ceiling), this averages to 400.0 grants/s at Location A and 399.7 grants/s at Location C, matching the same maximum established before. The 4G baseline, for comparison, measured the same way from the \gls{eNB} traces, grants 1000.0 and 992.5 subframes/s at the same two locations, respectively. This equates to a 2.5$\times$ reduction in uplink transmission opportunities ($1000.0/400.0 = 2.5$ at Location A, $992.5/399.7 = 2.48$ at Location C) between 4G and 5G performance, which no amount of signal quality could recover, since it is imposed before a single symbol is transmitted.

In the subsequent rounds of testing, the TDD pattern was rebalanced to a symmetrical 2:2 pattern, expected to improve uplink throughput at the cost of downlink throughput, making their performance almost equal, which Figure~\ref{fig:tdd-structure} presents. Only one configuration variable changes between each pair of rounds in the figure (the TDD pattern between rounds 1 and 2, and \texttt{rx\_gain} between rounds 2 and 3) so the two steps should be read as evidence for two separate effects rather than a single continuous trend.

The pattern used from Round~2 onward, shown in Listing~\ref{lst:tdd-22-cfg} of Chapter~\ref{cha:methodology}, doubles \texttt{nof\_ul\_slots} to 2 within the same 5-slot, 2.5\,ms period, raising the structural ceiling to $2\times400=800$ uplink transmissions per second. Round~2 metrics data was extracted directly from srsRAN's native timestamped scheduler log rather than the periodic console dump used in Round~1, so the raw evidence takes a different shape: Listing~\ref{lst:ul-800-grant-ceiling} shows an excerpt of the relevant uplink fields from three consecutive one-second \texttt{gnb.log} entries for the Location A TCP uplink test (the full lines also report downlink and per-HARQ statistics, omitted here for space).

This is a direct consequence of the adjustment in the TDD pattern, which is what the grant rate alone predicts.

We see on the figure that initially, as expected, when using the 3:1 TDD pattern, the uplink throughput on both Location A and C was significantly lower than the downlink throughput. On Round 2, after rebalancing the TDD pattern to 2:2, the uplink performance rose to nearly match the downlink in Location A, surpassing the downlink throughput at location C. We also note that the downlink throughput decreased in both locations, as expected.

The same airtime accounting also predicts the downlink share should fall $\times0.69$ of its Round 1 value ($\times0.75$); the observed downlink throughput fell further still, to $\times0.56$ at Location A and $\times0.44$ at Location C, so some factor beyond airtime alone also affected the downlink.

Finally, on Round 3, after adjusting the \texttt{rx\_gain} to 60: at Location A (2\,m), the overall network throughput increased significantly, as this change decreased the number of \texttt{ovl} events on the uplink, thus stabilizing the network's performance. However, at Location C, further away from the radio, the uplink throughput decreased significantly as well, due to the lower \texttt{rx\_gain} desensitizing the receiver and reducing the link budget. This change was expected as a trade-off between the two locations, and it highlights the challenge of finding a single configuration that optimizes performance across all distances.

We also see that in this final configuration, the throughput between both directions is closest to being equal at short range: at Location A the two directions converge to within a narrow margin (22.4 vs.\ 25.2\,Mb/s), the best balance observed anywhere in the dataset, while at Location C the \texttt{rx\_gain} reduction gives back most of the uplink gain Round 2 had recovered. This Location C behaviour is the trade-off associated with the \texttt{rx\_gain} change.

\section{Uplink quality vs distance: SINR, PHR, and the \texttt{rx\_gain} trade-off}
\label{sec:uplink-quality-rxgain}

Section~\ref{sec:tdd-rebalance} showed that Location C's uplink gave back most of the gain the 2:2 TDD rebalance had recovered once \texttt{rx\_gain} was lowered to 60 in Round 3, while Location A improved further still. This section looks at the link-quality data behind that split outcome: how uplink SINR and transmit power headroom evolve with distance, why the receiver gain setting used throughout Rounds 1 and 2 hurts the network precisely where the signal is strongest, and why no single fixed gain value serves the whole cell.

\subsection{Round 1: the uplink degrades with distance in 5G but not in 4G}

Averaged over the active window of each test, 5G's uplink SINR falls sharply as distance increases, while 4G's stays essentially flat, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:sinr-vs-distance}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{4G mean SINR} & \textbf{5G mean SINR} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m, LOS) & +13.7\,dB & +10.7\,dB \\
        \hline
        B (5\,m, partition) & +14.2\,dB & +7.1\,dB \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m, concrete wall) & +11.5\,dB & $-$7.6\,dB \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Round 1 mean uplink SINR by location, 4G vs 5G.}
    \label{tab:sinr-vs-distance}
\end{table}

Between Locations A and C, free-space path loss alone accounts for 14\,dB, yet 4G's uplink SINR barely moves (11.5--14.2\,dB, a spread of under 3\,dB), while 5G loses 18\,dB over the same distance and becomes markedly noisier location to location. The explanation is in the power headroom report (PHR), which states how much transmit power the handset has left above what it is currently using, shown in Table~\ref{tab:phr-vs-distance}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{4G PHR} & \textbf{5G PHR} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m, LOS) & +20.0\,dB & $-$2.0\,dB \\
        \hline
        B (5\,m, partition) & +16.6\,dB & $-$10.3\,dB \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m, concrete wall) & +12.3\,dB & $-$13.6\,dB \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Round 1 mean uplink power headroom by location, 4G vs 5G.}
    \label{tab:phr-vs-distance}
\end{table}

The 4G handset keeps 12--20\,dB of transmit power in reserve at every location, so closed-loop power control can simply push harder as path loss grows and hold SINR roughly constant, which is exactly the flat curve in Table~\ref{tab:sinr-vs-distance}. The 5G handset, by contrast, has \emph{negative} headroom already at Location A, in clear line of sight and at the shortest distance tested: it is power-limited at the easiest point in the test matrix and has nothing left to spend as distance increases, so every additional decibel of path loss lands directly on the received SINR instead of being absorbed by the power control loop.

Inverting the 3GPP TS 38.213 \S7.7.1 Type-1 PHR equation with the values read out of the logs (a $p_{0\text{-NominalWithGrant}}$ of $-76$\,dBm from SIB1, a mean PUSCH allocation of 42 PRB, 30\,kHz SCS and $\alpha=1$) gives the implied uplink path loss at each location, shown in Table~\ref{tab:phr-implied-loss}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{Measured PHR} & \textbf{Implied path loss} & \textbf{Free-space loss} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m) & $-2.03$\,dB & 81.8\,dB (+32.7\,dB excess) & 49.1\,dB \\
        \hline
        B (5\,m) & $-10.34$\,dB & 90.1\,dB (+33.0\,dB excess) & 57.1\,dB \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m) & $-13.55$\,dB & 93.3\,dB (+30.2\,dB excess) & 63.1\,dB \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Implied uplink path loss from Round 1 PHR, assuming a UE maximum transmit power ($P_{\text{CMAX}}$) of $+23$\,dBm.}
    \label{tab:phr-implied-loss}
\end{table}

The implied loss exceeds free-space loss by a near-constant $\sim$33\,dB at all three locations. Because this excess does not scale with distance, it cannot be a propagation effect; something in the link budget or calibration is consuming roughly the same amount of margin everywhere.

An earlier version of this analysis attributed that excess to a transmit-power cap (\texttt{pcg\_p\_nr\_fr1: -15}) present in the checked-in \texttt{gnb.yaml}. This explanation does not hold: the gNB's own startup log for this campaign prints its full non-default configuration on boot, and that dump has no \texttt{pcg\_p\_nr\_fr1} line, which means the parameter was unset for the entire test campaign, not just left at a value that happened not to bind. The measured PHR and the $\sim$33\,dB excess in Table~\ref{tab:phr-implied-loss} are real; the cause is not this cap, and remains open. The leading, unconfirmed candidate is a miscalibration between the declared \texttt{ss-PBCH-BlockPower} and \texttt{p0-NominalWithGrant} values in the cell configuration and the actual RF chain in use (antenna, cabling, and a \texttt{tx\_gain} of 89.75, the SDR's maximum): both parameters feed directly into the UE's open- and closed-loop path-loss estimate, and if either was left at a value taken from a reference configuration rather than calibrated against this testbed's hardware, the UE would systematically mis-estimate path loss by a roughly constant offset at every location, which is the same signature attributed to the (nonexistent) power cap. This has not been verified against a calibrated reference and is reported here as an open question, not a conclusion. What is established independently is that the receive gain chain was already pushed to its limit throughout Round 1 and Round 2 (\texttt{tx\_gain} 89.75, \texttt{rx\_gain} 70, against 50/45 in the reference configuration), a choice that reproduces receiver overload at short range, examined next.

\subsection{Round 2: link adaptation over-reaches near the radio at \texttt{rx\_gain 70}}

The 2:2 TDD rebalance in Round 2 (Section~\ref{sec:tdd-rebalance}) left \texttt{rx\_gain} unchanged at 70. Table~\ref{tab:overload-evidence} shows the steady-state uplink at each location under that setting: the grant rate the scheduler achieves, the SINR and MCS it reports, and the block error rate (BLER) actually observed.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{Grants/s (ok/failed)} & \textbf{SINR} & \textbf{MCS} & \textbf{BLER} & \textbf{PHR} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m, LOS) & 800 (619/181) & 20.5\,dB & 18.5 & \textbf{22\%} & $-2.0$\,dB \\
        \hline
        B (5\,m, partition) & 761 (369/391) & 14.2\,dB & 17.0 & \textbf{53\%} & $-13.2$\,dB \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m, concrete) & 800 (787/13) & 18.0\,dB & 12.1 & \textbf{1.3\%} & $-16.2$\,dB \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Round 2 (\texttt{rx\_gain 70}) uplink grant rate, reported SINR, chosen MCS, and measured BLER, steady state.}
    \label{tab:overload-evidence}
\end{table}

Three details in this table only fit together one way. First, the scheduler grants close to the full 800 uplink slots/s the 2:2 pattern allows at every location, so the earlier airtime ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:tdd-rebalance}) is not what limits Location A or C here; what differs between them is how many of those grants decode successfully. Second, the gNB reports a similar, comfortable SINR at both ends of the range, 20.5\,dB at 2\,m and 18.0\,dB at 10\,m, yet the measured BLER is 22\% at the near location and only 1.3\% at the far one: within each location BLER falls as SINR rises, as it should, but across locations the relationship inverts. Third, the difference tracks the MCS that link adaptation selects: an aggressive MCS 18.5 at Location A against MCS 12.1 at Location C. At 10\,m the scheduler is conservative relative to an apparently honest SINR reading and gets a low error rate; at 2\,m it picks a much higher MCS against a SINR reading that is evidently optimistic by something in the region of 6--10\,dB, and roughly one uplink block in five fails.

A SINR estimate that is inflated specifically at high received power is the signature of front-end compression: distortion products introduced by an overloaded receiver front end are not distinguished from genuine signal by the estimator, so the reported SINR stays high while the underlying constellation is already distorted. Two pieces of supporting evidence back this reading. The \texttt{ovl} flag, srsRAN's explicit receiver-overload marker, fires in 7.2\% of reports at Location A, 1.9\% at Location B, and never at Location C, an ordering that tracks received power exactly. Separately, a manual gain sweep in the lab found heavy overload at \texttt{rx\_gain 70} and a clean receiver at \texttt{rx\_gain 60} at the closest location. The \texttt{ovl} flag does not, however, fire inside the uplink test windows themselves (only during downlink tests), so hard ADC saturation during the uplink runs is not directly observed; the case for front-end compression here rests on the SINR/BLER inversion and the ordering of the overload flag, and should be read as a well-supported inference rather than a proven mechanism.

\subsection{Round 3: the \texttt{rx\_gain} sweep, and the limit of a fixed gain setting}

Round 3 re-ran the same locations and TDD pattern with \texttt{rx\_gain} lowered to 60. Table~\ref{tab:rxgain-sweep} compares uplink throughput, SINR, and BLER between the two gain settings, holding placement and TDD configuration fixed.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Location} & \textbf{\texttt{rx\_gain 70} (Round 2)} & \textbf{\texttt{rx\_gain 60} (Round 3)} \\
        \hline
        A (2\,m, LOS) & 15.9\,Mb/s, 20.5\,dB, 22\% BLER & \textbf{29.3\,Mb/s} ($\times$1.8), \textbf{25.3\,dB}, \textbf{6\% BLER} \\
        \hline
        B (5\,m, partition) & 5.9\,Mb/s, 14.2\,dB, 52\% BLER & 7.8\,Mb/s ($\times$1.3), 9.9\,dB, \textbf{2\% BLER} \\
        \hline
        C (10\,m, concrete) & 15.6\,Mb/s, 18.0\,dB, 1\% BLER & \textbf{2.6\,Mb/s} ($\times$0.17), \textbf{1.0\,dB}, 5\% BLER \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Uplink throughput, SINR, and BLER at \texttt{rx\_gain 70} vs \texttt{rx\_gain 60}, mean of TCP and UDP, same placement and TDD pattern.}
    \label{tab:rxgain-sweep}
\end{table}

At Location A, cutting the receive gain by 10\,dB removed the front-end compression identified in the previous subsection: SINR rose by 4.8\,dB in real terms, block errors fell fourfold, and throughput nearly doubled to 29.3\,Mb/s, at this point roughly twice the 4G baseline at the same location. At Location C, the same 10\,dB gain cut cost 17\,dB of SINR, more than one-for-one, because the link went from sitting comfortably above the noise floor to sitting on it; throughput collapsed to 0.17$\times$ its Round 2 value. Location B sits between the two, gaining throughput and losing badly on SINR while BLER still falls, which is consistent with a link that starts out marginal rather than saturated by front-end compression.

One loose end from this sweep is worth closing before moving on: PHR at \texttt{rx\_gain 60} read close to 0\,dB during the lab gain sweep at Location A but $-$10 to $-$12\,dB during the full Round 3 campaign at the same gain and placement. This is not a hidden accumulator effect or a sign that the gain setting itself drifted; the PHR formula in TS 38.213 \S7.7.1 includes a $-10\log_{10}(M_{RB})$ term, so headroom falls as the scheduler grants wider allocations, and the lab sweep was carried out on an essentially idle link while the Round 3 campaign ran saturated transfers with allocations around 46 PRB. Binned by PRB count, measured PHR across the Round 3 dataset tracks the predicted $-10\log_{10}(M_{RB})$ term to within about 1.5\,dB (correlation $-0.64$ over 1331 reports), which fully accounts for the difference: the practical consequence is that a PHR figure is only comparable to another one taken at a similar allocation size.

Taken together, these three rounds point to a single, well-evidenced limitation rather than a fixable bug: with a fixed, manually-set receiver gain and no automatic gain control, this gNB cannot serve the whole cell. \texttt{rx\_gain 70} is correct at 10\,m and overloads at 2\,m; \texttt{rx\_gain 60} is correct at 2\,m and desensitises at 10\,m. There is no single value that works, and the two campaigns bracket the same problem from opposite sides. This also retroactively explains the non-monotonic distance behaviour seen in both campaigns, and generalises beyond this testbed: it is a consequence of running a software RAN on a commercial-off-the-shelf SDR without per-UE receive-gain control, not an artefact of this specific configuration.
