\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{cha:state-of-the-art}

The deployment of 5G networks using \gls{OSS} has already become an area of research, with many researchers having explored the feasibility of deploying 5G networks using open-source components. Very different approaches have been taken, from small-scale testbeds to complex environments integrated with commercial equipment. This chapter groups the relevant works surveyed by the kind of question they set out to answer. Section~\ref{sec:related_single_testbed} covers works that build and report on a single, self-contained 5G SA testbed, with a particular focus on \gls{UE} integration. Section~\ref{sec:related_comparative} covers works that instead compare multiple open-source implementations or deployment approaches directly against one another. Section~\ref{sec:related_automation_scale} covers works concerned with automation and with scale, from continuously-tested lab deployments to real-world and production networks. Section~\ref{sec:related_overview} closes the chapter with a comparative overview of the reviewed testbeds, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:related_works_comparison}. Most of these works were sourced from srsRAN's website, under the Research tab, complemented by a small number of additional papers found through a broader literature search.

\section{Single-Testbed Deployments and UE Integration}
\label{sec:related_single_testbed}

\textcite{tio2025exploring5g} implemented a complete 5G \gls{SA} testbed using Open5GS for the Core and srsRAN for the \gls{gNB}, deployed on an Intel i7-12700 host with two \glspl{SDR}, a high-end USRP X310 and a mid-range USRP B200, operating on band n3 (FR1). The Core network was deployed in containers, via Docker Compose. Their main contribution lies in comparing three fundamentally different \gls{UE} implementations side by side: srsUE, srsRAN's own 5G \gls{UE} software-defined implementation; a \gls{COTS} Samsung Galaxy A33 5G handset, provisioned with a programmable SIM card from the Open Cells project; and a Sixfab 5G HAT (built around a Quectel RM502Q-AE modem) mounted on a Raspberry Pi 4, an intermediate option offering more configurability than a \gls{COTS} device without the fully virtualized paradigm of srsUE.

Across the four bandwidth configurations tested, the \gls{COTS} handset consistently delivered the highest downlink throughput, peaking at 68.6 Mbps at 20 MHz, with the Sixfab HAT close behind at 63.0 Mbps and srsUE trailing at 20.8 Mbps. Combining multiple \glspl{UE} simultaneously exposed connection problems: pairing the \gls{COTS} device with the Sixfab HAT roughly halved each device's individual throughput, a predictable outcome of sharing the radio medium, but adding srsUE to the mix depressed the other two devices' throughput by over 75\%, an effect the authors attribute to srsRAN's resource allocation behaviour and the high RF gain srsUE's USRP B200 link required. Internet-facing Speedtest measurements (via Ookla) were more consistent for the \gls{COTS} and Sixfab devices, upwards of 20.1 Mbps, whereas srsUE showed an unexplained throughput peak at 10 MHz followed by a drop at higher bandwidths.

Qualitative real-world tests, such as package downloads through \texttt{apt}, video streaming on YouTube, and video calls over Zoom, further differentiated the three \glspl{UE}: the \gls{COTS} handset handled every test cleanly; the Sixfab HAT stayed connected throughout but could not render video calls reliably, a limitation of the Raspberry Pi's browser rather than the radio link; and srsUE, while functional, suffered frequent YouTube buffering problems and could not sustain a stable video call. The authors conclude that \gls{COTS} devices remain the strongest option for raw performance, but that low-cost, low-power alternatives such as the Sixfab HAT are viable for \gls{IoT} and portable deployment scenarios.

On a similar vein to the previous work, \textcite{vazquez2024maq5g} too developed a complete 5G \gls{SA} testbed, named ``MAQ5G''. Their approach relies solely on the OpenAirInterface project, deploying both their 5G Core and \gls{RAN} software on USRP X310 and B210 \glspl{SDR}, with some modifications to the \gls{gNB} scheduler's code.

Although similar, the evaluation done in this work shines a bigger light on the radio performance, measuring the throughput through different means of transmission and \gls{RAN} configurations, using two \glsxtrshort{COTS} devices fitted with programmable SIM cards. The authors also evaluated the \gls{RAN} host system's performance under different traffic loads, measuring CPU usage.

Results showed that their testbed was able to provide connectivity to the \glspl{UE} and was able to achieve a maximum downlink throughput of 149 Mbps, although they also observed that, under high traffic loads, the host system's behaviour became unreliable, leading to loss of connection for the \glspl{UE}, with the only remedy being a full \gls{UE} reboot.

\textcite{mamushiane2023deploying} took a markedly more practical angle, positioning their work as a deployment guideline rather than a performance study. Their testbed paired srsRAN, chosen over \gls{OAI} for its stronger documentation and community support, with Open5GS, deployed on a workstation (Intel Core i9-9900, 64\,GB RAM) connected to a National Instruments NI-2944R USRP over PCIe, with the Core network itself running inside Docker containers on an OpenStack virtual machine. The paper's main contribution is a detailed account of the practical obstacles involved in attaching consumer-grade \glspl{UE} to a 5G SA network: out of four \gls{COTS} handsets tested (a Xiaomi Redmi Note 11 Pro, a Huawei P40 Pro, a Samsung S21, and a Huawei 5G Router CPE), only the Huawei P40 Pro and a Samsung Galaxy S-series device attached successfully, with connection requiring a \gls{GPSDO} external clock reference for gNB/\gls{UE} synchronisation, a 30\,kHz \gls{SCS} for TDD compatibility, and custom-programmed SIM cards (Sysmocom and Open-Cells) carrying authentication parameters matched to the Core's subscriber database. The authors further worked through the mechanics of network slice selection from the \gls{UE}'s perspective, distinguishing the Configured, Subscribed, Requested and Allowed slice-selection states, and provided a two-part troubleshooting guide at the end, covering RAN and Core errors they encountered along the way.

While not the central focus of their work, the authors also captured a \gls{gNB} packet trace under minimal RF optimisation (30\,kHz \gls{SCS}, 100 resource blocks), which showed almost perfect link conditions, with at most one to two packets dropped per second on the downlink and negligible loss on the uplink.

\section{Comparative Evaluations of Open-Source Implementations}
\label{sec:related_comparative}

Closer to methodological concerns, \textcite{rouili2024evaluating} asked a different question altogether: how much does the choice of \gls{RAN} deployment approach, between real hardware, emulation and pure simulation, actually matter for the realism of the results? Using a common \gls{OAI} 5G Core, the authors built and benchmarked three \gls{RAN} scenarios against one another: a full \gls{SDR} deployment (an \gls{OAI} \gls{gNB} driving a USRP X310, connected to a \gls{COTS} Google Pixel 7 Pro over the air, on band n78 with 40 MHz bandwidth); \gls{OAI}'s RFsimulator, which emulates the radio interface between a simulated \gls{UE} and the \gls{gNB} while still exercising the full protocol stack; and UERANSIM, which removess the \gls{PHY} layer entirely and simulates a UDP-based radio link.

Measured against the 3GPP-derived theoretical throughput ceiling for their configuration (122 Mbps), the \gls{SDR} deployment reached 95\% of that ceiling, RFsimulator only around 60\%, while UERANSIM, unconstrained by any \gls{PHY}-layer bottleneck, achieved the full 1 Gbps saturated link, an unrealistically high result that reflects the absence of any radio-layer resource allocation rather than true \gls{RAN} performance. The gap was just as pronounced under load: idle round-trip latency measured 15 ms for the \gls{SDR} deployment and 7 ms for RFsimulator, both being influenced by frame alignment, HARQ retransmissions and channel conditions, against under 1 ms for UERANSIM. Under sustained traffic, latency rose to roughly 220 ms and 250 ms respectively for the \gls{SDR} and RFsimulator scenarios, while UERANSIM again showed negligible change. Resource consumption followed a different, almost inverted pattern: the \gls{PHY}-layer processing of the \gls{SDR} deployment consumed up to 94\% of a CPU core, and RFsimulator's software-based signal generation peaked even higher at around 140\% (spread across more than one core), whereas UERANSIM never exceeded roughly 30\%. The authors conclude that RFsimulator and, especially, UERANSIM are useful for functional and protocol-level testing, but systematically misrepresent the throughput, latency and resource costs a real radio deployment incurs, a finding that reinforces the value of the \gls{SDR}-based, real hardware approach this dissertation's own testbed relies on.

Shifting focus towards containerization and orchestration, and the complexity of managing heterogeneous software stacks, \textcite{horstmann2025open5gcube} developed open5Gcube, a modular framework that unifies the deployment and management of multiple open-source mobile network project combinations, rather than committing to a single Core/\gls{RAN} pairing as the previous works do, able to build 2G, 4G and 5G \gls{SA}/\gls{NSA} networks from the same infrastructure.

Their framework wraps each project's services (Open5GS, Free5GC and \gls{OAI} Core for the Core network, srsRAN and \gls{OAI} \gls{RAN} for the base station, and OsmoBTS for legacy 2G) in Docker containers organised into use-case specific ``stacks'', configured through shared Docker Compose templates and driven by a script based orchestrator built specifically to avoid the operational overhead the authors found unjustified in Kubernetes or Docker Swarm for a laboratory-scale deployment. The hardware backbone is a portable 19-inch rack housing two USRP X310 \glspl{SDR} and space for USRP B210s, synchronised via a Leo Bodnar \gls{GPSDO} reference clock, the same device family used in this dissertation's project testbed.

The evaluation is the most extensive \gls{UE} compatibility study among the related works surveyed here, covering a Quectel RM500Q-GL modem and six \gls{COTS} smartphones: a Samsung Galaxy S25, a OnePlus 13, a Morotola Edge 50 PRO, a iPhone 13 Mini, an Asus ROG Phone 6 and a Google Pixel 9, tested against every combination of Core and \gls{RAN} implementation. All six devices connected reliably for 2G and basic 4G data; results diverged sharply once VoLTE and 5G SA were tested. Under 4G VoLTE (srsRAN plus Open5GS, with Kamailio providing the IMS), most devices registered successfully, but one handset repeatedly dropped its connection seconds after attaching due to a missing IMS configuration. Under 5G SA, the Samsung Galaxy S25, OnePlus 13, Asus ROG Phone 6 and the Quectel modem connected and sustained a two-minute flood-ping without any connection loss across every Core/\gls{RAN} combination tested; the Google Pixel 9, by contrast, could not see the cell at all when paired with srsRAN, and connected to the \gls{RAN} but failed to establish a \gls{PDU} session when paired with \gls{OAI} \gls{RAN} and Free5GC; and the iPhone required manual \gls{SUCI} concealment configuration on its SIM and would not attach at all to \gls{OAI} Core, which lacks \gls{SUCI}-based authentication support. The authors conclude that, while basic connectivity is broadly achievable across the open-source ecosystem, compatibility with advanced features remains handset- and implementation-specific, reinforcing the value of testing against the specific \gls{UE} models a deployment is meant to serve.

Focusing specifically on the Core network rather than the \gls{RAN}, \textcite{barbosa2025opensource} conducted the most direct head-to-head comparison among the surveyed works, evaluating Open5GS, Free5GC and \gls{OAI} Core (a fourth platform, SD-Core, was excluded from the practical evaluation as it only supports Kubernetes-based deployment) against a shared \gls{RAN}: an \gls{OAI} \gls{gNB} driving an Ettus B210 \gls{SDR} on band n78, connected to a \gls{COTS} handset over the air. Each Core implementation ran containerized on the same server, isolating the Core software itself as the only variable between test runs.

For control-plane procedures, Open5GS was consistently the fastest, registering \glspl{UE} in 0.47\,s and establishing \gls{PDU} sessions in 0.28\,s, against 0.53\,s/0.31\,s for Free5GC and 0.66\,s/0.33\,s for \gls{OAI} Core, roughly 13--40\% slower depending on the procedure. The result is reversed on the data plane: \gls{OAI} Core delivered the highest throughput, with 100.1 Mbps downlink and 4.8 Mbps uplink, with the lowest latency (12.7 ms)of all three, around 50\% higher throughput than Free5GC, which came last on every data-plane metric (66.5 Mbps downlink, 3.11 Mbps uplink, 14.14 ms latency), with Open5GS in between. Together with the resource-consumption results reported alongside these measurements, where Free5GC drew the least computing required, the authors conclude that no single open-source Core platform dominates across all axes: the right choice depends on whether a deployment prioritises signaling responsiveness, data-plane throughput, or resource efficiency, a trade-off directly relevant to platform selection for any new open-source 5G testbed.

\section{Automation, Scale, and Real-World Deployments}
\label{sec:related_automation_scale}

% 5G-CT
Integrating \glsxtrshort{CD/CI} and automation tools, \textcite{bonati2025_5gct} presented "5G-CT", a framework focused on automated deployment and \gls{OTA} testing. Their network consisted of an Open5GS core, an A5G Networks core and OpenAirInterface \gls{RAN}, deployed at a Northeastern University lab, in Massachusetts.

Their work stands out due to the tools they employed: OpenShift, a Kubernetes cluster where the Core and \gls{RAN} components would be deployed, and GitOps workflows, the automation tool used to orchestrate the deployment of the network. By using these tools, they automated the provisioning of their 5G networks, effectively streamlining the setup and guaranteeing a consistent and reproducible deployment process.

To show the capabilities of their framework, they conducted a series of tests over the course of roughly nine months. During this period, the framework executed an automated cycle every six hours (four times a day), instantiating the \gls{gNB} and Core, connecting a Sierra Wireless EM9191 5G modem as \gls{UE}, and exchanging downlink UDP traffic at one of three target data rates (10, 20 and 30 Mbps). In the end, the framework would report the results to dedicated data-collector pods running on OpenShift, where they would be analyzed and stored, before the network was torn down.

Results over the nine-month campaign showed that the 10 and 20 Mbps configurations sustained steady throughput on almost every run, while the 30 Mbps configuration was less stable, showing intermittent throughput dips and the associated packet loss the authors report (visualised only for this worst-case configuration, for clarity). The authors traced this instability to a combination of factors, bugs in the \gls{OAI} code, gNB-to-\gls{SDR} communication issues, interference, and Linux kernel scheduling behaviour, which was substantially mitigated by an update to the host kernel configuration, midway through the testing campaign, after which even the 30 Mbps runs stabilised. Beyond raw throughput, 5G-CT also compares each new test statistically against a confidence interval built from the full history of prior runs at the same configuration, and is only flagged as successful if it falls within that historical envelope, letting the framework detect performance regressions dynamically, rather than relying on a fixed threshold. The framework also kept the \gls{gNB} software itself continuously up to date, checking weekly for new \gls{OAI} releases and rebuilding its container image whenever one appeared.

%Ericsson RAN
While these works focus on \gls{OSS} implementations using \glspl{SDR}, \textcite{zu2025realworld} bridged the gap between \gls{OSS} and commercial applications, focusing on the realism and the scale of real-world deployments. Unlike the testbeds discussed elsewhere in this chapter, built from scratch around open-source \gls{RAN} software, their contribution is a real-world application. The ARA Wireless Living Lab, a real scale wireless lab spanning Iowa State University's campus, the City of Ames, and surrounding research farms and rural communities across a 30\,km radius, had for years run on Ericsson's own native 5G core (E-5GC). The authors report on transitioning the already deployed, commercial grade \gls{RAN} over to Open5GS, and on the resulting performance differences between the two cores.

The \gls{RAN} itself is composed of 4 Ericsson \glspl{gNB}, each split into three sectors, with every sector carrying both a mid-band AIR 6419 radio and a millimetre-wave AIR 5322 radio, connecting to more than 30 \gls{UE} sites equipped with Quectel RG530 modems, scattered across residential areas, university research farms and commercial buildings. The original E-5GC deployment split its control and user planes geographically, where the control plane ran out of an Ericsson lab in California, while the user plane sat locally at the ARA data center in Iowa, the two connected over a nationwide 100\,Gbps fibre backbone. Migrating to Open5GS brought both planes onto a single Dell R750 server (Intel Xeon Gold 6342, 395\,GB RAM, 100\,Gbps NICs) co-located with the \glspl{gNB}, and it is this architectural change, more than the core software change itself, that the authors credit for most of the latency improvement they measured.

Performance-wise, the transition came at a measurable but acceptable cost. Open5GS reduced average registration and service-request latency by 19.1\,ms and 63.0\,ms respectively, credited to the now local control/user-plane location, while \gls{PDU} session establishment latency improved only marginally (1.7\,ms), which the authors attribute to inefficiencies in Open5GS's own network functions. On the user plane, Open5GS reached 445.6 Mbps in a short-range farm field test, around 20\% below the roughly 557 Mbps the commercial E-5GC achieved under the same conditions (but still several times over the $\sim$100 Mbps \gls{eMBB} requirement it needs to meet). Across a broader set of field sites, downlink throughput averaged closer to 300 Mbps, with over 90\% of measurements exceeding 200 Mbps, demonstrating that an open-source 5G Core can reliably sustain carrier-grade performance alongside a commercial \gls{RAN} in real-world conditions.

The authors also ran a parallel comparison against UERANSIM, the same \gls{RAN} simulator benchmarked by \textcite{rouili2024evaluating} in Section~\ref{sec:related_comparative}: swapping the field \gls{UE} for a simulated one connected to the same Open5GS core roughly tripled measured throughput (978.6 Mbps versus 299.1 Mbps) and cut registration latency by an order of magnitude (14.1\,ms versus 142.0\,ms), since the simulator bypasses real channel impairments entirely. This independent confirmation of the same simulation/reality gap reinforces the case for the real, \gls{SDR}-based approach this dissertation's own testbed takes.

Stepping outside the lab entirely and differing on the Core, \textcite{hasan2023building} reported on Magma not as an experimental testbed but as a production system, describing lessons learnt from real-world deployments that diverge sharply from the campus and lab scale setups of the other works surveyed here. Magma's core design decision, terminating radio-specific protocols at an access gateway placed close to the radio rather than deep in the Core, lets a single control plane and management platform serve heterogeneous, geographically dispersed sites regardless of the underlying radio technology (5G, LTE, Wi-Fi or others). The authors report deployments spanning more than 800 eNodeBs across 45 US states at the time of writing, alongside smaller-scale examples such as a solar powered rural LTE site built with Magma for an independent ISP in Peru, and quantify cost savings of around 43\% in one deployment compared to a traditional carrier core architecture. 

While Magma's production focus and its emphasis on Wi-Fi/LTE heterogeneity rather than 5G SA sets it apart from the rest of this chapter (and, being a production case study rather than a fixed lab configuration, it does not map onto the comparison in Table~\ref{tab:related_works_comparison}), it demonstrates that the open-source approaches surveyed here are not confined to research prototypes, and can scale to genuine commercial deployments.
